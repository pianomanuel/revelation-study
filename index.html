Orientation: four reading strategies

Preterist: Most of the visions are fulfilled in the first century: the fall of Jerusalem (AD 70) and/or Rome's persecution of the church.

Historicist: The visions map onto church history from the apostles to the end (classic Protestant reading, e.g., the papacy as the beast).

Futurist: Chapters 4-22 describe events still to come before and after Christ's return. Dispensational futurism (Evans's school) adds a pretribulation rapture, a seven-year tribulation, Israel's national restoration and a literal 1,000-year reign, and often uses 1:19 as a three-part outline (past: ch. 1; present: chs. 2-3; future: chs. 4-22).

Idealist: The visions portray the timeless conflict between God and evil, applicable in every age.

Eclectic: Beale (NIGTC) calls his own position a 'redemptive-historical form of modified idealism'. Osborne (BECNT) and Mounce (NICNT) combine futurist and other elements. Koester (Anchor Yale) reads the book chiefly in its first-century setting with ongoing application. Bauckham focuses on its theology and its critique of Rome.

Genre: Revelation calls itself an apocalypse (apokalypsis, 1:1), a prophecy (1:3; 22:7, 18-19) and a circular letter (1:4-5; 22:21). Bauckham and Aune both stress this mixed genre. Read it as a letter to seven real churches that uses apocalyptic imagery to deliver prophetic exhortation.

Structure: Three numbered septets: seals (6:1-8:5), trumpets (8:6-11:19) and bowls (15:1-16:21). Interludes sit between the sixth and seventh items (ch. 7; 10:1-11:14). Recapitulationists, beginning with Victorinus of Pettau (3rd century) and including Beale, read the septets as parallel views of the same period. Futurists such as Evans tend to read them sequentially, or as 'telescoping', with the seventh seal containing the trumpets and the seventh trumpet the bowls. Bauckham (The Climax of Prophecy) traces a deliberate numerical design, for example 'Lamb' (arnion) appearing 28 times (7 x 4).

Date and Roman context: Irenaeus (Against Heresies 5.30.3) places the vision near the end of Domitian's reign (c. AD 95). A minority dates it to Nero or shortly after AD 68. The background includes the imperial cult in Asia Minor, local trade guilds, synagogue-church tensions, and the Nero redivivus legend (compare 13:3; 17:8-11).
