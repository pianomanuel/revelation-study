Greek terms

salpinx (8:6): 'Trumpet'. In the OT, trumpets sound for war, festivals, the fall of Jericho (Josh 6) and the Day of the Lord (Joel 2:1; Zeph 1:16).

ouai, ouai, ouai (8:13): 'Woe, woe, woe'. The eagle's three woes cover trumpets 5, 6 and 7 (9:12; 11:14), which marks the last three as intensified.

to triton ('a third'): Repeated throughout chapters 8-9. The judgments are partial compared with the bowls (all-encompassing, ch. 16): they are warning judgments meant to lead to repentance.

abyssos and phrear (9:1-2): 'The abyss' and 'the shaft'. In Jewish tradition (1 Enoch), the prison of fallen spirits. The key is given to the fallen star, so the demonic acts only under permission.

Abaddōn / Apollyōn (9:11): Hebrew 'Destruction' (Job 26:6; Prov 15:11) and Greek 'Destroyer'. Many (e.g., Aune, Koester) see a pun on Apollo, whose symbol included the locust and whom emperors such as Nero and Domitian associated themselves with.

pharmakeia (9:21): 'Sorcery', 'magic arts'. Linked to idolatry and Babylon's deception (18:23). The English 'pharmacy' derives from it, but the sense here is occult practice, not medicine.

chronos ouketi estai (10:6): Literally 'time will be no longer'. Modern translations correctly render it 'there will be no more delay' (NIV, NRSVue), answering the martyrs' 'how long?' (6:10).

to mystērion tou theou (10:7): 'The mystery of God', announced to the prophets (Amos 3:7) and fulfilled at the seventh trumpet (11:15).

biblaridion (10:2, 9-10): 'Little scroll'. Bauckham (The Climax of Prophecy) argues it is the same scroll as the Lamb's scroll of chapter 5, now opened and given to John to proclaim. Others (e.g., Aune) treat it as distinct.

dei se palin prophēteusai (10:11): 'You must prophesy again' about peoples, nations, languages and kings: a renewed commission introducing 11:1-13.

Old Testament allusions

Trumpets 1-5 mirror the Exodus plagues: hail and fire mixed with blood (Exod 9:23-26), water to blood (Exod 7:20-21), darkness (Exod 10:21-23) and locusts (Exod 10:12-15). The falling star 'Wormwood' recalls Jer 9:15 and 23:15 (bitter water as judgment). The locust army comes from Joel 1-2 (teeth like lions, sound of chariots, darkened sun). The golden altar and censer (8:3-5) echo Lev 16:12 and Ezek 10:2. The Euphrates as the boundary from which invaders come recalls Jer 46:10 and Isa 8:7. 10:5-7, the angel raising his hand to swear by the Creator, is a direct echo of Dan 12:7. 10:9-10, eating the scroll, sweet as honey, reworks Ezek 2:8-3:3 (and Jer 15:16). 10:11 echoes Jeremiah's commission over nations (Jer 1:10).

Roman context

9:13-19: cavalry from beyond the Euphrates calls up Rome's dread of the Parthians, the empire's only undefeated rival on its eastern frontier, famed for mounted archers who shot backwards. This fits the horses' power 'in their tails' (9:19). Readers in Asia also knew the Nero redivivus rumor that Nero would return leading Parthian armies. The trumpets' natural disasters (earthquake, darkened sky, poisoned water) would recall the Asia Minor earthquakes and the eruption of Vesuvius (AD 79).

Interpretive schools

Dispensational futurist (Evans; inferred from his published dispensational premillennial stance, not quoted from the videos): Literal future judgments during the tribulation, falling in sequence after the seals. The locusts are literal or demonic beings, and the 200 million army a future invasion, sometimes linked by popular dispensationalists to modern Asian armies. Check Evans's own wording before attributing any specific identification to him.

Historicist: Classic Protestant readings took the locusts as the rise of Islam (Saracens) and the Euphrates horsemen as the Ottoman Turks.

Preterist: Judgments on Jerusalem leading up to AD 70, or on Rome.

Idealist and recapitulation (Beale): The trumpets cover the same period as the seals (the whole church age) from a different angle. Demonic deception (9:1-11) and death (9:13-19) fall on those who reject God, and like the Exodus plagues they harden rather than convert. Bauckham emphasizes that the trumpets' failure to bring repentance (9:20-21) sets up chapter 11: what judgment cannot do, faithful witness will (compare 11:13, where the survivors 'gave glory to the God of heaven').

Textual notes

8:13: the best manuscripts read aetou ('eagle'). Some later manuscripts, followed by the Textus Receptus and the KJV, read angelou ('angel'). 9:12-13: a few witnesses omit 'four' in 'the four horns of the golden altar'. NA28 (the standard critical Greek text) brackets it as uncertain. 10:4: the seven thunders are sealed, the only revelation in the book John is told not to write. This underlines that God's plan includes elements deliberately withheld, a caution against over-detailed end-times charts.

Conclusions

The trumpets show God's judgment as both real and restrained ('a third'). It is aimed at repentance, but judgment alone does not produce it (9:20-21). Chapter 10 pivots from the plagues to proclamation. The decisive question is how the nations will come to repentance, and the answer (ch. 11) is the costly prophetic witness of God's people. For every interpretive school, the application is the same: bitter-sweet faithfulness to the word, and urgency because 'there will be no more delay'.

If asked

Are the trumpets literal? Futurists read literal future judgments; others read the imagery, modelled on the Exodus plagues, as symbolic of judgments that recur through history. Every reading notes that they are partial ('a third') and aim at repentance, which does not come (9:20-21). Evans (inferred from his dispensational stance): literal future judgments following the seals.

Who are the locusts and the 200 million? Many read the locusts as demonic powers (they come from the abyss and have a king, Abaddon). The cavalry draws on Rome's fear of the Parthians beyond the Euphrates. Popular futurists have linked the 200 million to a modern Asian army; most scholars see symbolic, demonic armies. Evans (inferred from his dispensational stance): literal or demonic forces in the tribulation; the specific Asian-army link is not confirmed for him.

What is the little scroll? Some identify it with the Lamb's scroll of chapter 5 (Bauckham), now opened and given to John to proclaim; others see a separate commission. Eating it echoes Ezekiel 3: the message is sweet to receive and bitter to deliver.

Steering questions: Why did judgment not lead to repentance (9:20-21)? What turns the story toward witness in chapter 10?

Discussion questions

Men's group (Monday)

1. The survivors 'did not repent' (9:20-21) even after judgment. What patterns have you excused despite clear warning?

2. 9:21 lists murders, sorceries (pharmakeia), sexual immorality and theft. Which of these does our culture market to men most aggressively?

3. John eats the scroll: sweet, then bitter (10:9-10). When has obeying God's word cost you something?

Co-ed group (Tuesday)

1. Even under severe warning, people 'did not repent' (9:20-21). Why is repentance so hard for individuals, families and nations?

2. The trumpets echo the Exodus plagues. What does it mean that God's judgments are also meant to free his people?

3. John found God's word sweet and bitter (10:9-10). When has a hard truth from Scripture turned out to be good for you?
