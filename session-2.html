Greek terms

meta tauta (4:1): 'After these things'. A visionary transition formula. Dispensationalists read 'come up here' as a type of the rapture; most commentators read it as John's own prophetic ascent (compare Ezek 3:12-14).

thronos (4:2 and following): 'Throne'. Used 17 times in chapters 4-5 and over 40 times in the book. The central question of Revelation is whose throne is real: God's or Caesar's.

hagios, hagios, hagios (4:8): The trisagion of Isa 6:3, joined to 'the one who was and is and is to come'. The creatures' worship is ceaseless ('day and night they never rest').

axios (4:11; 5:2, 4, 9, 12): 'Worthy'. Axios ei was an acclamation used for emperors at their arrival; John reserves it for God and the Lamb.

biblion (5:1): A scroll written 'inside and on the back' and sealed with seven seals. Readings include a Roman will (sealed by seven witnesses), a title deed (Jer 32:10-14), or the scroll of Ezek 2:9-10. It contains God's plan of judgment and redemption.

ho leōn ho ek tēs phylēs Iouda, hē rhiza Dauid (5:5): 'The Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David'. Two messianic titles from Gen 49:9 and Isa 11:1, 10.

arnion hōs esphagmenon hestēkos (5:6): 'A Lamb standing as though slaughtered'. The perfect participle esphagmenon means the marks of slaughter remain. Arnion is used 28 times of Christ (7 x 4, Bauckham).

ēgorasas (5:9): 'You ransomed' or 'purchased'. Market language for buying slaves, here applied to people from 'every tribe and language and people and nation', the book's fourfold formula for all humanity.

Old Testament allusions

Chapter 4 fuses the throne visions of Ezek 1 (living creatures, rainbow, crystal sea; compare Ezek 1:22, 26-28) and Isa 6:1-3 (six wings, the trisagion), with Dan 7:9-10 (thrones, fire, the court). The four creatures' faces (lion, ox, human, eagle) come from Ezek 1:10. The 'sea of glass' (4:6) may echo the bronze sea of Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 7:23) or the waters above the firmament (Gen 1:7). The sealed scroll (5:1) recalls Ezek 2:9-10 and the sealed book of Dan 12:4, 9: what Daniel was told to seal, the Lamb now opens. Gen 49:9-10 and Isa 11:1, 10 supply the Lion and Root; Isa 53:7 the lamb led to slaughter; Exod 12 the Passover lamb whose blood redeems. 5:10, a kingdom and priests, repeats Exod 19:6 (compare 1:6). The 'new song' (5:9) echoes Pss 33:3, 96:1, 98:1 and Isa 42:10.

Roman context

The heavenly court mirrors, and contests, the imperial court. Client kings laid crowns (stephanoi) before the emperor as a sign of submission, as Tiridates of Armenia did before Nero (Tacitus, Annals 15.29). Hymns acclaimed emperors as 'worthy' and 'lord and god' (dominus et deus noster, Suetonius, Domitian 13.2). The 24 elders may also evoke the 24 lictors who attended Domitian. The cosmic acclamation of 5:13 includes 'every creature' and thus every emperor. Aune's 'The Influence of Roman Imperial Court Ceremonial on the Apocalypse of John' (Biblical Research 28, 1983) is the classic study.

Interpretive schools

Dispensational futurist (Evans; inferred from his published dispensational premillennial stance, not quoted from the videos): Chapter 4 begins the future section of the book (1:19), with 'come up here' (4:1) picturing the church's rapture before the tribulation, since the word 'church' does not appear again until 22:16. The 24 elders are commonly read as the raptured, crowned church. The scroll is the title deed to the earth, which Christ reclaims.

Preterist: The scroll is God's covenant lawsuit or judgment against unfaithful Jerusalem (or against Rome); the throne vision commissions what follows in the first century.

Historicist: The scroll's seals unfold successive eras of history from the apostolic age onward.

Idealist and modified idealist (Beale, Bauckham): Chapters 4-5 are the interpretive key to the whole book: the slain Lamb's enthronement after his death and resurrection (compare Dan 7:13-14) begins the fulfilment of God's plan now. The elders represent the whole people of God (12 tribes plus 12 apostles, compare 21:12-14) or an angelic council. The silence about the church after chapter 3 reflects a change of genre, not the church's absence.

Textual notes

4:11: the best witnesses read 'they existed and were created' (ēsan kai ektisthēsan, A, 046), which reverses the expected order; some manuscripts smooth it to 'they are'. 5:9: Codex Alexandrinus omits 'us' (tō theō without hēmas), and NA28 follows it, so the NRSVue and NIV read 'ransomed for God saints from every tribe'. The Textus Receptus (KJV) reads 'redeemed us', which made the elders human. 5:10 accordingly reads 'made them' and 'they will reign', not 'us' and 'we'.

Conclusions

The throne room governs everything that follows. Chapter 4 establishes God as Creator, worthy because 'you created all things'. Chapter 5 adds redemption: the Lamb is worthy because he was slaughtered. The central reversal of the book occurs here: John hears of a conquering Lion and sees a slaughtered Lamb, so messianic victory is redefined by the cross. Worship widens in three circles (the creatures and elders, myriads of angels, every creature), and the Lamb receives the same worship as God (5:13), Bauckham's strongest evidence for Revelation's high Christology. Whatever one's eschatology, the church's first response to history is worship, not calculation.

Discussion questions

Men's group (Monday)

1. The elders cast their crowns before the throne (4:10). What 'crowns' (titles, achievements, provider identity) do men find hardest to lay down?

2. John hears 'Lion' and sees a slaughtered Lamb (5:5-6). How does that redefine strength and leadership for a man?

3. How does 4:11 (God worthy as Creator) speak to a man's sense of control over his work and future?

Co-ed group (Tuesday)

1. Which line of the throne-room worship (4:8, 4:11, 5:9-10, 5:12) would you most want to sing this week, and why?

2. 5:9-10 says Christ ransomed people 'from every tribe and language'. How does our group reflect that, and how could it more?

3. How does the Lion-who-is-a-Lamb image shape how we use influence in our homes and friendships?
