Greek terms

pistoi kai alēthinoi (22:6): 'Trustworthy and true'. These words are reliable, the formula used of the rider (19:11) and of 21:5.

en tachei / tachy (22:6, 7, 12, 20): 'Soon', 'quickly'. Repeated four times; the book closes with urgency.

makarios (22:7, 14): 'Blessed'. The sixth and seventh beatitudes, matching the first (1:3): blessed is the one who keeps the words.

proskynēsai (22:8-9): 'To worship'. John falls at the angel's feet a second time (compare 19:10) and is rebuked: 'Worship God!'

mē sphragisēs (22:10): 'Do not seal up'. A reversal of Dan 12:4: the time is near.

to alpha kai to ō (22:13): 'The Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End'. Three titles God uses for himself (1:8; 21:6) are now spoken by Jesus.

hoi plynontes tas stolas autōn (22:14): 'Those who wash their robes'. Present tense: ongoing faithfulness (compare 7:14).

erchou (22:17, 20): 'Come!' The Spirit and the bride invite the thirsty to come, and the church prays for Christ to come: the same imperative both ways.

amēn, erchou kyrie Iēsou (22:20): 'Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!' The Greek equivalent of the Aramaic Marana tha (1 Cor 16:22; Didache 10.6), one of the earliest Christian prayers.

Old Testament allusions

22:12 echoes Isa 40:10 and 62:11 (his reward is with him) and Prov 24:12 (repaying according to works). 22:13 draws together Isa 41:4, 44:6 and 48:12 ('I am the first and I am the last'), so Jesus speaks as YHWH. 22:16, 'the root and the offspring of David', returns to Isa 11:1, 10; 'the bright morning star' to Num 24:17 (a star from Jacob). 22:17 adapts Isa 55:1 (water without price). 22:18-19 echoes Deut 4:2 and 12:32 (neither add nor take away) and Deut 29:20 (the curses written in this book). The command not to seal the book (22:10) reverses Dan 8:26 and 12:4, 9. 22:11 ('let the evildoer still do evil') draws on Dan 12:10.

Roman context

Ancient authors protected their books with integrity curses against copyists who altered them (compare the Letter of Aristeas 311; Irenaeus' adjuration at the end of On the Ogdoad, quoted by Eusebius, Church History 5.20.2). The closing 'grace' (22:21) is the language of a Christian letter (compare the end of Paul's letters), so the book closes as a letter to be read aloud in worship (1:3), probably at the Lord's Supper, where the prayer 'Come, Lord' was used (Didache 10.6). The 'dogs, sorcerers, sexually immoral, murderers, idolaters' outside the city (22:15) name sins associated with pagan cult and the imperial order.

Interpretive schools

Dispensational futurist (Evans; inferred from his published dispensational premillennial stance, not quoted from the videos): 'Soon' expresses imminence: Christ's return (the rapture) could happen at any moment, though the 'soon' of 22:20 need not mean within John's lifetime. The warning against adding to or taking away from the prophecy is usually applied to the whole canon. Expect Evans to close with an evangelistic invitation from 22:17.

Preterist: 'Soon' means what it says: the coming in judgment occurred in the first century (AD 70), with a final coming still future in partial-preterist versions.

Historicist: The book's predictions span history to the end; 'soon' signals that the sequence has already begun.

Idealist and modified idealist (Beale, Bauckham): 'Soon' expresses the inaugurated eschatology of the whole New Testament: the end has begun in Christ's death and resurrection and may come at any time. The book's closing frame (prologue 1:1-8 and epilogue 22:6-21) defines it as a prophecy that calls for response now.

Textual notes

22:14: the earliest witnesses (Aleph, A) read 'wash their robes' (plynontes tas stolas autōn); the Textus Receptus (KJV) reads 'do his commandments' (poiountes tas entolas autou). The phrases look nearly identical in uncial script. 22:19: the Textus Receptus 'book of life' arose from Erasmus's back-translation from the Latin Vulgate (whose manuscripts had libro for ligno), since his only Greek manuscript lacked the final verses; every Greek manuscript reads 'tree of life'. 22:21: endings vary between 'with all', 'with all the saints' and 'with you all'. NA28 reads 'with all'.

Conclusions

The book closes as it opened: a liturgical letter, with beatitudes, a warning and grace. The epilogue frames everything: these are words to be kept, not merely decoded. Three movements close the book: Christ's promise ('I am coming soon'), the church's prayer ('Come, Lord Jesus') and the gospel invitation ('Let everyone who is thirsty come'). The warning of 22:18-19 protects the message from distortion by addition (speculation) and subtraction (dismissal). The last word is grace.

Discussion questions

Men's group (Monday)

1. Jesus says 'I am coming soon' three times. How would you live this week if you took that seriously?

2. 'Let everyone who is thirsty come' (22:17). Which man in your life needs that invitation?

3. 22:18-19 warns against adding to or taking away from the prophecy. How do we handle speculation about the end responsibly?

Co-ed group (Tuesday)

1. 'Come, Lord Jesus' (22:20): is that prayer easy or hard for you right now?

2. Who would you most like to invite with 22:17, 'Let everyone who is thirsty come'?

3. What is one way this study of Revelation has changed how you see the future?
