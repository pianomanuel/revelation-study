Greek terms

kainos (21:1, 2, 5): 'New' in quality, not merely recent (neos). 'I am making all things new' (21:5) suggests renewal of the present creation rather than replacement (compare Rom 8:21).

hē thalassa ouk estin eti (21:1): 'The sea was no more'. The sea is the source of the beast (13:1), the realm of chaos and the trade routes of Babylon (18:17-19).

skēnē / skēnōsei (21:3): 'Tent', 'tabernacle'; 'he will dwell'. Compare John 1:14 ('he tabernacled among us') and the Shekinah of God's presence.

laoi autou (21:3): 'His peoples' (plural, Aleph, A). The covenant formula is widened to many peoples, reflecting Zech 2:11.

gegonan (21:6): 'It is done', 'They have come to pass'. Compare 16:17 and John 19:30.

ho nikōn klēronomēsei (21:7): 'The one who conquers will inherit these things'. The final overcomer promise, gathering the promises of chapters 2-3.

tetragōnos (21:16): 'Square' or 'foursquare'. The city is a cube 12,000 stadia (about 1,400 miles) on each side, matching the shape of the Holy of Holies (1 Kgs 6:20).

naos (21:22): 'Temple', the inner sanctuary. 'I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb.'

therapeia tōn ethnōn (22:2): 'The healing of the nations'. The nations are not only judged but healed.

Old Testament allusions

21:1 takes up Isa 65:17 and 66:22 (new heavens and a new earth). 21:2 draws on Isa 52:1 and 61:10 (Jerusalem as bride). 21:3 joins the covenant formula of Lev 26:11-12, Ezek 37:27 and Zech 2:10-11. 21:4 quotes Isa 25:8 and 35:10; 'I am making all things new' (21:5) echoes Isa 43:19. The water of life without price (21:6) comes from Isa 55:1. The city's description (21:9-22:5) is modelled on Ezek 40-48: measured by an angel, with twelve gates named for the tribes (Ezek 48:30-35). The jewels of the foundations (21:19-20) recall the high priest's breastpiece (Exod 28:17-21) and Isa 54:11-12. Kings and nations bringing their glory (21:24-26) and gates never shut (21:25) come from Isa 60:3, 5, 11, 19-20. The river of life and the trees with healing leaves (22:1-2) are Ezek 47:1-12, and the tree of life and the end of the curse (22:2-3) reverse Gen 2-3 and Zech 14:11.

Roman context

The bride-city replaces the prostitute-city of chapters 17-18. Babylon drew wealth from the world by trade and coercion; the new Jerusalem receives the glory of the nations freely (21:24-26). Its gates stand open, unlike Rome's walled imperial order. Roman cities advertised themselves with gold, marble and monumental gates; here the whole city is gold, each gate a single pearl, and the main street gold 'transparent as glass'. The promise of no more tears, death or mourning addresses communities that buried martyrs and lived amid plague and earthquake.

Interpretive schools

Dispensational futurist (Evans; inferred from his published dispensational premillennial stance, not quoted from the videos): After the millennium and the final judgment, God creates a literal new heaven and earth. The new Jerusalem is a literal city, many dispensationalists add, perhaps hovering above the earth, and the distinction between Israel (the gates) and the church (the foundations) continues in eternity. Evans likely emphasizes the believer's eternal reward and reign with Christ; check his treatment.

Preterist: The new Jerusalem is the new covenant people of God that replaced the old Jerusalem after AD 70, already present in part.

Historicist: The final vision follows the end of history; most historicists read it as the eternal state.

Idealist and modified idealist (Beale, Bauckham, Koester): The city is the people of God described as a place, the bride. The cube means the whole city is a Holy of Holies where all God's people dwell in his immediate presence; there is no temple because the whole creation has become God's dwelling. Most commentators favour renewal of the present creation over its annihilation.

Textual notes

21:3: the plural 'peoples' (laoi, Aleph, A) is better attested than the singular 'people' (laos), which scribes assimilated to OT usage. A further phrase, 'and be their God' (autōn theos), is uncertain. 22:2: the syntax is disputed: 'in the middle of its street, and on either side of the river' or 'in the middle of its street. On either side of the river is the tree of life.' 21:24: the Textus Receptus adds 'of those who are saved' ('the nations of them which are saved'), a scribal limitation absent from the earliest manuscripts.

Conclusions

The goal of the whole story is not escape from the world but God dwelling with his people in a renewed creation: 'See, the home of God is among mortals.' Every threat of the earlier chapters is removed (sea, death, tears, curse, night) and Eden is restored and surpassed: a garden-city with the tree of life open to all. The nations who were deceived by Babylon now walk by the city's light and are healed. The promises to the overcomers in chapters 2-3 are fulfilled. The highest blessing is the last: 'they will see his face' (22:4).

If asked

Is the new Jerusalem a literal city? Some futurists expect a literal cubic city about 1,400 miles on each side. Most scholars read the city as the people of God (the bride, 21:9-10) and the cube as an enlarged Holy of Holies (1 Kgs 6:20), meaning all God's people live in his presence. Evans (inferred from his dispensational stance): a literal city, the eternal home of the redeemed.

Will this world be destroyed or renewed? 'I am making all things new' (21:5) and kainos ('new in quality') lead many to favour renewal of the present creation (compare Rom 8:21); others cite 2 Pet 3:10 and 21:1 ('passed away') for replacement. Christians differ.

Is there no sea in heaven? The sea in Revelation represents chaos, the source of the beast (13:1), and Babylon's trade routes. Its absence means the end of evil and danger, not a geographical detail.

Steering questions: What is restored here from Genesis 2-3? How does God dwelling with us change how we live now?

Discussion questions

Men's group (Monday)

1. 21:4 promises no more tears. What loss are you carrying that this verse speaks to?

2. There is no temple because God and the Lamb are its temple (21:22). How does God's presence change your view of ordinary work?

3. 'Nothing unclean will enter' (21:27). How does that shape the way you fight temptation now?

Co-ed group (Tuesday)

1. 21:3-5 is often read at funerals. Which promise here comforts you most, and why?

2. The city's gates never close and the nations bring their glory in (21:25-26). What does that say about God's welcome?

3. The tree of life's leaves are 'for the healing of the nations' (22:2). Where do you long to see healing?
