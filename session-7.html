(18:4) Then I heard another voice from heaven say: “‘Come out of her, my people,’ so that you will not share in her sins, so that you will not receive any of her plagues;

NRSV Updated Edition NRSVue

(18:4) Then I heard another voice from heaven saying, “Come out of her, my people, so that you do not take part in her sins and so that you do not share in her plagues,

Greek: phialē ('bowl', a libation vessel); pornē ('prostitute'); Babylōn hē megalē ('Babylon the great'); emporoi ('merchants').

OT allusions: Exod 15 (Song of Moses, 15:3-4); Exodus plagues (bowls); Jer 50-51 (Babylon's fall, 'come out of her', Jer 51:45); Ezek 26-27 (lament over Tyre, the model for chapter 18's dirges).

Roman context: The seven hills (17:9) identify Rome. 18:11-13 lists Rome's luxury imports, ending with 'slaves, that is, human souls'. See Bauckham, 'The Economic Critique of Rome in Revelation 18' (The Climax of Prophecy).

Interpretive schools: Some dispensationalists identify Babylon with a rebuilt literal Babylon or a future world system or religion. Most scholars read Rome, and by extension every idolatrous economic and political order. 16:16 'Armageddon' (Har-Magedon, 'mountain of Megiddo') is likely a symbolic place name.

Conclusions: The call to 'come out' (18:4) is primarily ethical and economic, not geographic. Heaven's 'Hallelujahs' (19:1-5; the only NT occurrences of the word) celebrate justice for the victims of empire.

Discussion questions

Men's group (Monday)

1. Rome's cargo list ends with 'human souls' (18:13). Where does our economy still trade in people?

2. 'Come out of her, my people' (18:4). Name one practical way to 'come out' this month.

3. The merchants weep for lost profit, not for the people (18:11). How do we keep money from shaping our grief and joy?

Co-ed group (Tuesday)

1. Babylon seduces with luxury (18:12-14). Which of our comforts would be hardest to give up for Christ?

2. What would it look like for our households to 'come out of her' (18:4) in spending, media or time?

3. Heaven's Hallelujahs celebrate justice (19:1-2). How do we hold justice and mercy together?
