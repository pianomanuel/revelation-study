Greek terms

hē ōdē Mōuseōs ... kai hē ōdē tou arniou (15:3): 'The song of Moses ... and the song of the Lamb'. One song, celebrating a new exodus. Its words are drawn entirely from OT texts.

phialē (15:7; 16:1 and following): 'Bowl'. A shallow libation or incense bowl used in temple worship (5:8). The prayers of the saints and the wrath of God are carried in the same vessels.

dikaios ... alēthinai (16:5-7): 'Just ... true'. The 'angel of the waters' and the altar affirm the justice of the judgments: 'they shed the blood of saints ... you have given them blood to drink.'

Harmagedōn (16:16): 'Har-Magedon'. Hebrew 'mountain of Megiddo'. But Megiddo is a plain, not a mountain; the name may be symbolic, or a play on 'mountain of assembly' (har mō'ēd, Isa 14:13).

idou erchomai hōs kleptēs (16:15): 'See, I am coming like a thief'. The third of seven beatitudes interrupts the battle scene, echoing Jesus' teaching (Matt 24:43; 1 Thess 5:2).

hē pornē hē megalē (17:1): 'The great prostitute'. Prophetic language for cities that trade in idolatry and wealth (Nah 3:4 of Nineveh; Isa 23:16-17 of Tyre).

mystērion (17:5, 7): 'Mystery'. The name on her forehead is to be interpreted, not taken literally.

emporoi (18:3, 11, 15, 23): 'Merchants'. The great men of the earth; Babylon's commerce is itself part of her guilt.

hallēlouia (19:1, 3, 4, 6): Transliterated Hebrew 'praise Yah'. These are the only four uses in the New Testament.

Old Testament allusions

Chapter 15 is a new exodus: the sea of glass mixed with fire (15:2) recalls the Red Sea, and the Song of Moses (Exod 15) is sung in words from Ps 111:2, Deut 32:4, Jer 10:7 and Ps 86:9. The tent of witness filled with smoke (15:5-8) echoes Exod 40:34-35 and 1 Kgs 8:10-11. The bowls repeat the Egyptian plagues: sores (Exod 9:10), water to blood (Exod 7:17-21), darkness (Exod 10:21), frogs (Exod 8:2-6), hail (Exod 9:24). The drying of the Euphrates (16:12) alludes to Cyrus' capture of Babylon (Isa 44:27; Jer 50:38; 51:36). The prostitute's portrait draws on Jer 51:7 (golden cup), Ezek 16 and 23 (Jerusalem and Samaria as prostitutes) and Nah 3:4. 'Come out of her, my people' (18:4) quotes Jer 51:45 (compare Isa 48:20; 52:11). 18:2 echoes Isa 13:21 and 34:11-14. The dirges of kings, merchants and seafarers (18:9-19) are modelled on the lament over Tyre (Ezek 26-27), and the millstone (18:21) on Jer 51:63-64.

Roman context

The woman seated on 'seven mountains' (17:9) is unmistakably Rome, the city of seven hills (Virgil, Georgics 2.535; Horace, Carmen Saeculare). The dea Roma, the goddess personifying Rome, appeared on coins of Asia Minor seated on seven hills (a sestertius of Vespasian, AD 71). 'Babylon' as a code name for Rome after AD 70 appears also in 1 Pet 5:13, 4 Ezra 3 and 2 Baruch 11, since both cities destroyed Jerusalem's temple. The seven kings (17:10) have been matched to lists of emperors beginning with Julius Caesar or Augustus, but no list fits neatly. The cargo list of 18:11-13 matches Rome's luxury imports: gold, silver, pearls, purple, silk, citron wood, ivory, cinnamon, and finally 'slaves, and human souls'. Bauckham's study shows how precisely the list matches what Roman writers (Pliny the Elder, Natural History 12-13) condemned as excess.

Interpretive schools

Dispensational futurist (Evans; inferred from his published dispensational premillennial stance, not quoted from the videos): The bowls are literal, rapid final judgments at the end of the tribulation. Armageddon is a literal final battle in the Jezreel Valley. Babylon is variously a rebuilt city on the Euphrates (as some dispensationalists hold), or a future global religious (ch. 17) and economic (ch. 18) system centered in a revived Rome. Evans would likely also apply 'come out of her' to believers' present separation from worldly systems; confirm in the video.

Preterist: Babylon is first-century Jerusalem (which 'killed the prophets', 18:24; compare Matt 23:35-37), destroyed in AD 70; or else Rome, whose fall came later.

Historicist: Babylon is papal Rome, a dominant Reformation reading.

Idealist and modified idealist (Beale, Bauckham, Koester): Babylon is Rome in its own day and, by extension, every economic and political order that seduces with prosperity and is built on idolatry and exploitation. The bowls recapitulate the trumpets at full intensity (no longer 'a third'). 'Come out of her' calls for ethical and economic separation now, not geographical flight.

Textual notes

15:3: 'King of the nations' (ethnōn: A, 046, majority) versus 'King of the ages' (aiōnōn: P47, Aleph, C); NA28 and the NRSVue read 'nations', which fits the quotation of Jer 10:7, while the NIV text reads 'nations' as well. The Textus Receptus 'King of saints' (hagiōn) has almost no Greek support. 17:8: 'was, and is not, and is to come' (parestai) is a deliberate parody of the divine title of 1:4, 8. 18:3: 'have drunk' is uncertain: manuscripts read 'have fallen' (peptōkan) and 'have drunk' (pepōkan), differing in one letter.

Conclusions

The bowls bring God's justice to completion; the prayers of the saints, carried in the same bowls (5:8), are answered. Heaven declares the judgments 'just and true' because they answer the shedding of innocent blood. Chapters 17-18 expose Babylon as a seductive city whose wealth rests on violence and the trade in 'human souls'. Revelation's critique is moral and economic as much as religious: the kings, merchants and sailors mourn only their own losses. The call to 'come out of her' (18:4) asks God's people to refuse complicity. The Hallelujahs of 19:1-5 celebrate not destruction itself but justice for the victims.

If asked

What and where is Armageddon? Har-Magedon is Hebrew for 'mountain of Megiddo', a site of famous battles (Judg 5:19; 2 Kgs 23:29), though Megiddo lies in a plain. Futurists expect a literal battle in the Jezreel Valley; many scholars read a symbolic name for the final confrontation. Notably, no battle is described at 16:16; the victory comes in 19:11-21. Evans (inferred from his dispensational stance): a literal final battle in Israel.

Who is Babylon? Most scholars read Rome (seven hills, 17:9; 'Babylon' as a code for Rome in 1 Pet 5:13 and Jewish texts), and by extension every idolatrous economic and political order. Preterists sometimes argue for Jerusalem; historicists for papal Rome; some dispensationalists for a rebuilt city on the Euphrates. Evans (inferred from his dispensational stance): a future religious and economic world system.

Does 'Come out of her' mean physically leaving? Jer 51:45 commanded exiles to flee the literal city. In Revelation most read an ethical and economic call to refuse complicity with Babylon's sins, wherever one lives.

Steering questions: What makes Babylon attractive? What would 'come out of her' mean in our spending and time?

Discussion questions

Men's group (Monday)

1. Rome's cargo list ends with 'human souls' (18:13). Where does our economy still trade in people?

2. 'Come out of her, my people' (18:4). Name one practical way to 'come out' this month.

3. The merchants weep for lost profit, not for the people (18:11). How do we keep money from shaping our grief and joy?

Co-ed group (Tuesday)

1. Babylon seduces with luxury (18:12-14). Which of our comforts would be hardest to give up for Christ?

2. What would it look like for our households to 'come out of her' (18:4) in spending, media or time?

3. Heaven's Hallelujahs celebrate justice (19:1-2). How do we hold justice and mercy together?
